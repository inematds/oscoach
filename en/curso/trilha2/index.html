🪪 Identity — the soul file (CLAUDE.md)

The first file the OS reads. Who it is, whom it serves, what it never refuses—concise, under 30 lines, with a practical generation example at the end.

What is:

The Identity layer lives in the CLAUDE.md — the first file the harness reads, before any task.

Why learn:

It's the piece that changes behavior the most for the least effort; a good file gets the tone right on the first try.

Key concepts:

Core file · read first · Markdown · sets the tone.

What is:

You, the business, the market, current events, preferences, and what you should always/never see. RAW data does NOT go in.

Why learn:

Mixing identity with substrate bloats the file; knowing the limit keeps the soul concise.

Key concepts:

Stable context · always/never · identity ≠ data.

What is:

Keep the file under ~30 lines and cut anything that doesn't change behavior every time.

Why learn:

An bloated identity dilutes the signal and uses up the model’s attention; keeping it short is a design decision.

Key concepts:

< 30 lines · cut until it hurts · point > paste.

What is:

If the OS serves a team, identity includes roles, responsibilities, and limits for each function.

Why learn:

The same domain generates different identities depending on the audience; define “you or team” early.

Key concepts:

Who it's for · roles · limits by role.

What is:

A cheat sheet explaining when the OS should give the benefit of the doubt on your legitimate tasks.

Why learn:

Models are becoming more judgmental; anticipating refusals avoids friction in every session.

Key concepts:

Cheat sheet · benefit of the doubt · legitimate use only.

What is:

A global file with what's common to you; each OS says "always consult X" instead of repeating it.

Why learn:

As you grow from 1 to 5 OSes, centralizing what they share avoids the maintenance nightmare.

Key concepts:

Global file · point, don’t copy · 1 edit point.

What is:

A stranger reads the CLAUDE.md and describes the OS’s purpose + 1 thing it refuses.

Why learn:

"It’s good" is vague; the stranger test is an objective criterion for being done.

Key concepts:

Stranger test · describes purpose · cold chat.

What is:

A ready-to-use prompt that interviews you with 3 questions and writes a CLAUDE.md concise, with a refusal cheat sheet.

Why learn:

Reading about identity doesn’t replace writing one; the prompt becomes a real artifact in minutes.

Key concepts:

Copy-run · interview > dump · built-in done-check.

🗑️🔥 Substrate & Context — the flaming garbage truck

From the chaos of 500–5,000 files to injectable nuggets: organize before AI, gather tacit knowledge, and distill with a workhorse model.

What is:

You start with 500–5,000 chaotic files of various types; the Substrate turns chaos into a reference.

Why learn:

Most people plug AI into the chaos and get frustrated; accepting the chaos and dealing with it methodically is the differentiator.

Key concepts:

Substrate vs. context · 500–5,000 files · design.

What is:

Mini data engineering: sort by file type first, then by age (recent vs. old).

Why learn:

The OS's quality is limited by the Substrate's; organizing it first multiplies everything that follows.

Key concepts:

Mini data engineering · by type · by age.

What is:

Gather what’s missing to create the specialist of your dreams — from YouTube, experts, laws, lawyers.

Why learn:

What you already have is rarely enough; harvesting deliberately closes the gap.

Key concepts:

Harvesting · dream expert · external sources.

What is:

Practical knowledge, rarely written down: shortcuts, exceptions, “what goes wrong”—things a generic model doesn’t know.

Why learn:

It’s the tacit knowledge that makes the OS seem like an expert rather than a chatbot; it’s the non-generic test.

Key concepts:

Tacit vs. explicit · exceptions · non-generic.

What is:

Aggregate sources in a compendium.md (distilled reference) and playbooks (how to do X).

Why learn:

It’s the format that makes knowledge usable; a good playbook is almost a Skill.

Key concepts:

Compendium · playbook · goal question (done-check).

What is:

Folder raw/ (raw, kept) vs. synthesized/ (nuggets). Inject only the nuggets.

Why learn:

It’s the pattern that repeats in every domain; injecting raw material blows up the window and drives up costs.

Key concepts:

raw/ · synthesized/ · pantry vs. ready-made dish.

What is:

A cheap model (e.g., Gemini Flash) running in a loop across all files to distill nuggets.

Why learn:

Distilling by hand doesn't scale, and using a top-of-the-line model is wasteful; the cheap+loop pair makes the volume feasible.

Key concepts:

Workhorse · in a loop · human in the loop.

What is:

Sweeping prompt raw/ with the workhorse and writes one nugget per file in synthesized/.

Why learn:

It’s the operational heart of Substrato; generalized, it becomes the "knowledge harvester" skill.

Key concepts:

raw/ intact · 1 nugget/file · compendium.md.

🚧 Rules & Hooks — the Fences

Rule = strong suggestion; hook = deterministic. Reflex hooks, settings.json with write denied, PII pre-commit, and the worst-error done check.

What is:

A strong suggestion to the model — the “do not enter” sign. Nondeterministic: it can slip.

Why learn:

Knowing which rules are “soft” calibrates your confidence and saves the hooks for what must not fail.

Key concepts:

Rule · non-deterministic · good for preferences.

What is:

The locked door: always does it or never lets it happen, without depending on the model's judgment.

Why learn:

PII, money, and database writes can't depend on the model "remembering".

Key concepts:

Hook · always/never · where it hurts.

What is:

Move the "always/never" lines from the CLAUDE.md bloated for a folder rules/.

Why learn:

The foundation grows without becoming a mess: lean identity, auditable rules.

Key concepts:

Promote · always/never · move > duplicate.

What is:

A mechanism that triggers immediately: PII (SIN, credit card) uploaded to GitHub? Action blocked immediately.

Why learn:

The most costly errors are silent; the reflex turns “I hope not” into “it can’t happen.”

Key concepts:

Reflex hook · PII · reflex > memory.

What is:

Ban Bash write commands (e.g., never write to a table), unless explicitly overridden.

Why learn:

It’s where "never write here" stops being text and becomes a machine rule — high return.

Key concepts:

settings.json · permissions.deny · unless overridden.

What is:

A checker that runs before each commit and blocks the submission if it finds PII.

Why learn:

A backup without protection is a leak waiting to happen; pre-commit lets you version safely.

Key concepts:

Pre-commit · blocks the commit · + .gitignore.

What is:

Name the worst mistake in the domain, write it as a clear never-rule, and reinforce it with 1 deterministic reflex.

Why learn:

Actionable criteria in 5 minutes: the right question, at the right dose (text + hook).

Key concepts:

Worst mistake · never-rule · double fence.

What is:

One settings.json ready to deny write commands and add a PreToolUse hook (exit 2 blocks).

Why learn:

Turns fence theory into real protection in minutes—the "1 reflex" of the done-check.

Key concepts:

permissions.deny · PreToolUse · exit 2 · override.
